Over the past 21 days, the introduction of Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs) and shifts in cobalt and uranium resource extraction networks in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DR Congo) have entered a new phase in technology policy and international resource dynamics. Initially, a positive outlook—a "bullish" stance—prevailed, but recently, the situation has reversed into an "escalation," highlighting complex interests and potential risks.

**Changes in Policy Direction and Their Background**

The introduction of CBDCs was expected to promote the growth of the digital economy and increase financial inclusion. However, recent changes suggest that these technological advancements may cause new challenges through complex links with international resource markets. In particular, DR Congo's cobalt and uranium extraction networks are key elements of the global technology supply chain, and control over resources in this region significantly impacts inter-state competition and resource price fluctuations.

**Changes in Resource Extraction Networks**

DR Congo accounts for approximately 70% of the world's cobalt production, and its uranium resources also hold a significant position. As resource extraction networks in this region have recently changed, the interests of technology companies and nations are becoming increasingly intertwined. This change signifies more than just fluctuations in resource prices; it represents international political pressure and the realignment of economic strategies to secure resources.

**BIS and the International Context**

A recent report from the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) contains warnings regarding the interaction between CBDC implementation and resource extraction networks. The BIS points out that the spread of digital currencies could further highlight the economic vulnerabilities of resource-dependent nations, raising the possibility of increased instability in international resource markets. In this context, a complex dynamic is forming where CBDC implementation may either strengthen or conversely weaken the resource control of resource-rich countries like DR Congo.

**Conclusion**

The introduction of CBDCs and changes in DR Congo's resource extraction networks signal deep shifts in international resource dynamics and economic policy, moving beyond mere technological issues. The transition from the initial optimistic outlook to the current complex situation suggests that nations and corporations must be more cautious in finding a balance between resource security and the digital economy. Future trends will serve as critical indicators determining the future of the global economy and technology policy.
